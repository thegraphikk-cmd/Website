Accessibility means making sure everyone can use your website, including people with low vision, colour blindness, hearing loss or motor difficulties, and the many older visitors who simply find small, faint text hard to read. It's good for customers, good for SEO, and most of it costs nothing if you plan for it.

Why it matters for a business

  • More customers: millions of people in India live with a disability, and many more have age-related vision or dexterity issues.
  • Better for everyone: readable text and clear buttons help people using phones in sunlight or on the move.
  • SEO overlap: headings, alt text and clear structure also help Google understand your pages.
  • Standards: WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) is the widely used reference, and accessibility expectations for websites are growing in India and abroad.

1. Text people can actually read

  • Contrast: dark text on a light background (or the reverse), with enough difference. Light grey text on white is one of the most common problems. WCAG asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text.
  • Size: body text around 16px or larger, and pages that still work when people zoom in.
  • Line length and spacing: not too wide, with comfortable line spacing.
  • Don't put important text inside images, where screen readers and search engines can't read it.

2. Alt text for images

Alt text describes an image for people using screen readers, and appears if the image fails to load. Describe what matters: "Modular kitchen with white cabinets and quartz countertop" rather than "IMG_2041". Decorative images can have empty alt text so screen readers skip them.

3. Clear headings and structure

Use one H1 per page, then H2 and H3 headings in order. Screen reader users jump between headings to find what they need, much as sighted visitors skim. This also helps your SEO.

4. Links and buttons that make sense

  • Use descriptive link text: "See our pricing" instead of "Click here".
  • Make buttons big enough to tap easily, roughly finger-sized on mobile.
  • Don't rely on colour alone to show something is a link or an error.

5. Forms everyone can complete

  • Every field has a visible label, not just placeholder text that disappears.
  • Error messages explain what's wrong and how to fix it.
  • Use the right input types so phones show number keypads for phone and PIN fields.
  • Avoid CAPTCHAs that are hard to solve; use gentler spam protection where possible.

6. Keyboard navigation

Some people can't use a mouse. Try using your site with only the Tab and Enter keys. You should be able to reach every link, menu and button, and see clearly where you are on the page (the focus outline).

7. Video and audio

  • Add captions to videos; YouTube's auto-captions are a starting point, but check them.
  • Don't auto-play video with sound.
  • Avoid flashing animations, and keep motion subtle.

8. Language

Write in plain, simple language, and set the page language correctly in the code. If you serve customers in Tamil and English, see whether your website should be bilingual.

A five-minute check

  1. Open your site on a phone outdoors. Can you read everything?
  2. Zoom to 200%. Does the layout still work?
  3. Tab through the home page with the keyboard.
  4. Run a free automated check such as Lighthouse's Accessibility audit in Chrome.

Automated tools catch only some issues, but they're a good start. Building accessibility in from the beginning is much easier than fixing it later. It belongs on your launch checklist.
