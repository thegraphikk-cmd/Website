Most website disputes don't come from bad intentions. They come from vague agreements: "a website for ₹40,000" with no clear list of pages, features, revisions or deadlines. A short written agreement protects both you and your designer. Here's what it should cover.

Note: this is a practical checklist, not legal advice. For large projects, have a lawyer review the agreement.

1. Scope: exactly what you're getting

  • Pages: list them (Home, About, 4 service pages, Contact, Blog).
  • Features: contact forms, WhatsApp button, booking, online payments, blog, multiple languages.
  • Platform: WordPress, Shopify, custom code, and why.
  • For stores: number of products uploaded, payment gateway, shipping integration.
  • What's not included: content writing, photography, logo design, ads, SEO beyond basics.

If it isn't written down, assume it isn't included.

2. Content: who provides what, and when

Content is the most common cause of delays. Agree who writes the text, who supplies photos, and the deadline for handing them over. Many projects stall for weeks waiting for "the about us text". See what to prepare before hiring a designer.

3. Timeline and milestones

  • Design approval date, development, review, launch.
  • What happens to the timeline if feedback or content is late.

Typical timelines are in how long it takes to build a website.

4. Price and payment

  • The total price, and whether GST is included.
  • Milestones, for example 40% advance, 40% on design approval, 20% before launch.
  • Recurring costs: domain, hosting, email, apps, maintenance, and who pays them.
  • Rates for extra work outside the scope.

5. Revisions

How many rounds of design changes are included, and what counts as a revision versus a new request. "Unlimited revisions" sounds nice, but usually means vague scope and delays.

6. Ownership and access

  • The domain registered in your name.
  • Ownership of content, design and code transferring to you on final payment.
  • All logins (admin, hosting, domain, analytics) handed over at launch.

More in who owns your website.

7. Hosting, maintenance and support

  • Who hosts the site, at what cost, and whether you can move it.
  • A free support period after launch (for example 30 days of bug fixes).
  • Maintenance plan terms if you want one: updates, backups, small edits. See maintenance costs.

8. Quality basics

It's reasonable to state that the site will be mobile-friendly, use HTTPS, load reasonably fast, include basic SEO settings and be tested on common browsers. See our launch checklist.

9. If things go wrong

  • How either side can end the project, and what's paid for work done so far.
  • What you receive if the project stops midway.
  • Confidentiality, and how disputes will be resolved.

Keep it simple

A clear two-page agreement, or a detailed proposal you both sign, is far better than a long contract nobody reads. The goal is that both sides know exactly what will be delivered, when, and for how much. When comparing providers, see 12 questions to ask a web design company.
