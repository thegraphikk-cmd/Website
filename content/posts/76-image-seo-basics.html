Images are usually the heaviest part of a web page, and often the reason a site is slow. They can also bring visitors through Google Images and help Google understand your pages. A few habits make a big difference to both.

1. Resize before uploading

Phone and camera photos are often 4000+ pixels wide and several megabytes. Most website images are displayed at 1200 to 2000 pixels wide at most. Resize to roughly the largest size the image will appear (around 2000 px for full-width banners, less for thumbnails).

2. Compress and use modern formats

  • WebP (or AVIF) is usually much smaller than JPEG or PNG at the same visual quality.
  • Use JPEG/WebP for photos and PNG or SVG for logos and simple graphics that need sharp edges or transparency.
  • Avoid saving photos as PNG; they can be ten times larger than needed.
  • Aim for most images under about 200 KB, and hero images under about 300 KB.

Free tools like Squoosh or TinyPNG work well, and many platforms convert images automatically.

3. Descriptive file names

Rename IMG_20241105_123456.jpg to something like modular-kitchen-white-quartz-chennai.jpg. Use lowercase words separated by hyphens. It's a small signal, but it helps Google understand the image.

4. Write useful alt text

Alt text describes the image for screen-reader users and appears if the image fails to load. It also helps Google understand what's pictured.

  • Good: "Bride in red Kanjeevaram saree during the muhurtham ceremony".
  • Bad: "image1", or a string of keywords.
  • Decorative images (backgrounds, dividers) can have empty alt text.

More on this in accessibility basics.

5. Set width and height

Telling the browser an image's dimensions lets it reserve space before the image loads, so the page doesn't jump around (layout shift). Most modern platforms do this automatically; custom sites should include width and height attributes.

6. Lazy-load below the fold

Images further down the page can load only when the visitor scrolls near them. But don't lazy-load the main image at the top; it should load immediately, since it's often the page's largest element. See Core Web Vitals.

7. Put images near relevant text

Google uses surrounding text, headings and captions to understand images. A project photo placed next to a description of that project is more meaningful than a gallery of unlabelled images.

8. Use your own photos

Original photos of your work, team and premises build trust and are more likely to appear in image results than stock photos used on thousands of other sites. If you use stock images, make sure the licence covers commercial use.

Quick checklist before uploading

  • Resized to the display size
  • Compressed, ideally WebP
  • Descriptive file name
  • Helpful alt text
  • Placed near relevant text

Then test your page speed. Images are usually the quickest win.
